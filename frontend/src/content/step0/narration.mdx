<Beat id="overview-inputs">

Every neuron starts with its inputs. There are four of them drawn here, $x_1$ up to
$x_4$, but there is nothing special about four. A neuron takes as many as you hand it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-weights">

Each input gets its own weight. The weights are the part the model actually learns:
a large one means that input matters a lot, and a weight near zero means the neuron
has decided to more or less ignore it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-sum">

Multiply each input by its weight and add the results together. Thats a dot product,
and it is the only real arithmetic in a forward pass.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-bias">

Then we add one more number, the bias. It belongs to the neuron rather than to any
input, and it lets the neuron shift its output up or down on its own.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-sigmoid">

Finally the total goes through a nonlinearity. Here we use the sigmoid, which
squashes whatever you give it into the range 0 to 1, so the answer can be read as a
probability.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-why-nonlinear">

You might reasonably wonder why we bother with that last step. Without it the whole
thing is a weighted sum, and stacking weighted sums on top of each other just gives
you another weighted sum. A hundred layers would collapse straight back down to one.
The nonlinearity is what stops that collapse, and it is the defining characteristic
of neural computing.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-plan">

So thats the object we are building. For the rest of this we will write it in C++ a
piece at a time, and every piece starts with the maths it encodes.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-sigmoid-formula">

Here is the sigmoid written out properly.

$$
\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}
$$

Give it a large negative number and you get something just above 0. Give it a large
positive one and you get something just below 1. It approaches both ends without
ever arriving.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-sigmoid-code">

And here is that formula as C++. One line, and it reads the same way round as the
maths does: one over one plus $e$ to the minus $x$. Notice the type is `double`
everywhere, which we keep consistent across every file in the project.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-sigmoid-derivative">

The reason we reach for sigmoid is what happens when you differentiate it.

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dx} = \sigma(x)\bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr)
$$

The derivative is written entirely in terms of the value we already worked out on
the way forward, so the backward pass gets it for free. One thing worth noticing
while we are here: the steepest this curve ever gets is 0.25, at the marked point.
Stack a lot of these in a row and the gradient reaching the early layers becomes
very small, but thats a problem for another rung.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-dot-formula">

The other thing `math.hpp` owes us is the dot product.

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a_i b_i
$$

Pair the elements up, multiply each pair, add the lot together.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-dot-numpy">

If you have written any machine learning in Python, you know this as one function
call. Thats exactly the gap this project is trying to close. The call is easy to
reach for, and what follows is what it does underneath.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-dot-code">

Written by hand it is a loop and an accumulator. Both vectors come in as const
references, since we never change them and we would rather not copy them on every
single call. The size check earns its place too: two vectors of different lengths
would otherwise read off the end of the shorter one.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-init">

Now the neuron itself. When we build one, the weights start as small random numbers
from a gaussian, scaled by one over the square root of the number of inputs. That
scale is doing real work. It keeps the initial weighted sum small however many
inputs there are, which lands the sigmoid near the middle of its range where it is
steep, instead of out on a flat tail where it barely learns at all.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-forward">

The forward pass is the picture we drew at the start, in one line. Dot product, add
the bias, through the sigmoid. Both functions from `math.hpp`, each used once.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-loss-math">

Next we need to say how wrong an answer is. Write $p$ for the prediction and $y$ for
the true label, which is either 0 or 1.

$$
L = -\bigl[\, y \log p + (1 - y) \log (1 - p) \,\bigr]
$$

Only one of those two terms is ever alive, because the label switches the other one
off.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-loss-clamp">

Have a look at what that loss does as the model becomes confident and wrong: it
climbs without any bound at all. A prediction of exactly 0 or exactly 1 would hand
$\log$ a zero and poison the whole epochs average. The clamp on the highlighted line
is what keeps that from ever happening.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-derivative">

To train we want the derivative of the loss with respect to the raw sum $z$, the
value before the sigmoid. Differentiating with respect to the prediction gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial p} = -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} = \frac{p - y}{p\,(1 - p)}
$$

which looks like it is about to get awkward, because of that denominator.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-cancellation">

Then we chain it with the sigmoid derivative from earlier, $\frac{dp}{dz} = p(1-p)$.

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}
= \frac{p - y}{p\,(1 - p)} \cdot p\,(1 - p)
= p - y
$$

The denominator cancels completely. The gradient of a sigmoid fed into binary cross
entropy is the prediction minus the label, with nothing left to divide by and
nothing that can blow up.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-gradient-code">

Which is why the backward pass looks like this. The highlighted line is that $p - y$,
straight out of the cancellation. Have a look for a call to a sigmoid derivative in
here: there is not one, because the algebra already did that work. Doing the calculus
properly is what made the code short.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-update">

Update is gradient descent itself. For each weight, take a small step against its
gradient, then do the same for the bias. How big a step is set by the learning rate.

</Beat>

<Beat id="logreg-learning-rate">

That learning rate is worth getting right. Too small and each step barely moves the
weight, so you run out of epochs before you get anywhere. Too large and every step
jumps clean over the minimum and lands further away than it started.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-epoch">

`main.cpp` puts the pieces in a loop. One epoch is one gradient over the whole
dataset followed by one update, so the two highlighted lines are the entire training
algorithm. Everything else in there is printing.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-training-tiny">

Here it is running, on three points that a straight line can separate. The weights
are the line: they set its slope, and the bias slides it off the origin. Watch the
line move as those numbers change, because that is all training is.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-accuracy">

Accuracy counts how many it got right, taking anything at or above a half as a 1.
For a model this small we train and score on the same data, which is not how you
would evaluate anything real, though it is enough to show where this one runs out of
road.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-xor-points">

Which brings us to XOR. Four points, and the label is 1 when exactly one of the two
inputs is 1. The filled dots sit on one diagonal and the hollow ones on the other.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-xor-boundary">

A single neuron draws one straight line and answers by which side you land on. Here
is the best line anyone can draw through these four points. It gets three of them,
and the ringed one is beyond it. Tilt it, shift it, try whatever you like: some point
always ends up on the wrong side, so 75% is the ceiling.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-xor-training">

So this is what training on XOR looks like. The loss drops a little, then settles
around 0.6931, which is $\ln 2$, exactly the loss of a coin flip. One straight line
has run out of things it can say, and that is the reason Rung 1 adds a hidden layer.

</Beat>
