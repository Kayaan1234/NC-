<Beat id="overview-stack">

Step zero gave us one neuron, and one neuron draws one straight line. Here is what
happens when we stop at one and start stacking them. Every unit in a layer takes the
whole of the previous layer as its input, and hands its own output on. Thats a
multilayer perceptron.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-xor">

Here is the problem that forces it. XOR is four points, and the answer is one when
exactly one of the two inputs is on. Have a look at where they sit. No straight line
separates the filled points from the hollow ones, and a single neuron only ever gets
a straight line, so it tops out at seventy five percent.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-transform">

Now watch what a hidden layer does with them. It does not find a cleverer boundary.
It moves the points. Each one goes through $z = xW + b$ and then a tanh, and lands
somewhere new, and in that new arrangement a straight line works fine. Thats the
whole idea of a hidden layer: bend the space until the easy answer is the right one.

</Beat>

<Beat id="overview-plan">

So we need three things. A matrix to hold a batch of examples, a layer that knows how
to go forwards and backwards, and a stack of those layers. Thats the order we will
build them in.

</Beat>

<Beat id="matrix-why">

Step zero worked one example at a time, and a `std::vector<double>` was plenty. That
falls apart here for two reasons. There are now whole layers of weights rather than
one row of them, and we want to push many examples through at once. Both of those
want the same object, so we may as well write it properly: a matrix, one row per
example.

</Beat>

<Beat id="matrix-flat">

Here is the thing worth knowing about `Matrix`. The grid you picture is a fiction.
Underneath it is one contiguous run of doubles, and the element at row $i$, column
$j$ lives at `i * columns + j`. That is a choice rather than an accident. Contiguous
memory is what lets the inner loop run fast, and that flat index turns up in nearly
every function in the file.

</Beat>

<Beat id="matrix-matmul">

Matrix multiply is the operation everything else is built on. Row $i$ of the answer
is row $i$ of $A$ measured against every column of $B$.

$$
C_{ij} = \sum_{k} A_{ik} B_{kj}
$$

In code thats three nested loops, and the inner one over `k` is the dot product you
already wrote in step zero. It has not changed at all. We just run it once per output
element and keep the answers.

</Beat>

<Beat id="matrix-bias">

Adding the bias needs one small trick. The bias holds a single row, one number per
output, but the thing we are adding it to has one row per example in the batch. So we
broadcast: the same bias row gets added to every row of the matrix. That check that
`Bias.rows` is one is the whole of it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="matrix-inplace">

You will notice most functions in this file come in pairs, one with a trailing
underscore and one without. The underscore version writes into the matrix you handed
it. The other allocates a fresh one and leaves yours alone. It matters more than it
looks. Training runs this arithmetic thousands of times a second, and a heap
allocation on every step is the difference between MNIST finishing and MNIST
crawling.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-activations">

Step zero had one nonlinearity, the sigmoid. Here we get two more. Tanh is the
sigmoid stretched to run from minus one to one, and having outputs centred on zero
makes life easier for the layer after it. Its derivative is drawn underneath, because
the derivative is the half we actually spend our time on.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-deriv-trick">

Now look closely at how the derivative is written. It takes `a`, the value the
activation already produced, rather than the `x` that went in. For tanh,
$f'(x) = 1 - \tanh^2(x)$, and $\tanh(x)$ is just $a$, so the whole thing is $1 - a^2$.
For the sigmoid it comes out as $a(1-a)$, which is exactly the term that cancelled in
step zero. So once we have the output we never need the input again, and that is why
the forward pass bothers to hang on to it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-relu">

ReLU is the blunt one. Pass positives straight through, flatten negatives to zero.
Its derivative is one or zero, so gradients travel through a deep stack without
shrinking away, and it costs a comparison instead of an exponential.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-softmax">

MNIST has ten possible answers rather than two, so we need ten numbers that behave
like probabilities. Softmax exponentiates each one and divides by the row's total, so
they come out positive and summing to one. The line worth looking at is the first
one, before any of that: we subtract the largest value in the row.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-softmax-stability">

Here is why. $e^x$ overflows a double somewhere around $x = 710$, and logits get
large. Subtracting the row maximum changes none of the answers, because the same
constant appears on the top and the bottom of the fraction and cancels straight out.
What it does change is that the largest exponent is now $e^0$, so nothing can
overflow. Step zero clamped its probabilities away from zero and one for this same
kind of reason.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-crossentropy">

The loss is cross entropy, and the binary cross entropy you derived in step zero was
a special case of it all along. With one-hot labels only the true class has a
non-zero target, so the sum collapses to minus the log of whatever probability we
gave the right answer.

$$
L = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} \log p_{i,\,y_i}
$$

Confident and right costs almost nothing. Confident and wrong costs a lot.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-absorbed">

And here is the payoff, which is step zero's cancellation happening again with ten
classes instead of two. Differentiate the softmax and the cross entropy separately
and you get a mess of terms. Differentiate the composition and nearly all of it
cancels:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = p - y
$$

Predicted minus true, divided by the batch size. Thats the whole gradient function.
Two lines.

</Beat>

<Beat id="math-absorbed-why">

Its worth sitting with that for a second, because it is the same shape as something
you have already done. In step zero, the sigmoid paired with binary cross entropy
gave $p - y$. Here, softmax paired with cross entropy gives $p - y$. The pairing is
chosen for exactly that reason. Put a loss against an output layer it was not
designed for and you inherit every one of those terms.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-holds">

A layer owns six matrices and an enum. `weight` and `bias` are the parts that learn.
`grad_weight` and `grad_bias` are where the backward pass puts its answers, and they
are deliberately the same shape as the things they are gradients of, which we lean on
in a minute. Then `cache_input` and `cache_output`, which forward fills in and
backward reads. And notice the constructor scales the random weights by
$1/\sqrt{\texttt{fan\_in}}$, the same trick from step zero, now once per layer.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-forward">

Forward is three lines and you can read them straight off the maths. Multiply the
input by the weights, add the bias across every row, push the result through the
activation.

$$
a = f(xW + b)
$$

The other two lines are bookkeeping. It keeps the input it saw, and it reuses its own
output buffer rather than allocating a new one every batch.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-cache">

Those two caches are worth a moment, because they look like an optimisation and they
are really a requirement. Backward needs the input, because the gradient with respect
to the weights depends on what got multiplied by them. And it needs the output,
because as we just saw the activation derivatives are written in terms of `a`.
Neither one is recoverable once forward has returned.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-chain">

Backward is the chain rule, and it arrives with one thing handed to it already:
$\partial L / \partial a$, how much the loss cares about this layer's output. We want
three things out of it. How the weights should change, how the bias should change,
and what to pass back to the layer behind us. The first move is shared by all three.
Multiply by the activation derivative to get $\partial L / \partial z$, the gradient
just before the nonlinearity.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-gradw">

For the weights, $\partial L / \partial W = x^{\top} \delta$. And here is the honest
way to check that at this size. $x$ is batch by fan-in. $\delta$ is batch by fan-out.
The answer has to have the shape of `weight`, which is fan-in by fan-out. Transpose
$x$ and you get fan-in by batch, and that against $\delta$ gives fan-in by fan-out
exactly. There is only one arrangement of those two that works.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-gradb">

The bias gradient is a column sum, and that falls out of what forward did. We added
the same bias row to every example in the batch, so every example's error feeds back
into it. Summing down the columns is what gathering all of those contributions
looks like.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-shapes">

Then the gradient we hand backwards, which is $\delta W^{\top}$. Same argument again.
It has to come out shaped like this layer's input, which is the previous layer's
output, and transposing the weights is the only thing that gets you there. In step
zero you could check the calculus by staring at it. At this size you check the
shapes, and shapes are hard to get wrong once you insist on them.

</Beat>

<Beat id="layer-update">

Update is step zero's line, applied to a whole matrix instead of a handful of
numbers. Scale the gradients by the learning rate, subtract them from the weights,
and thats it. It scales in place, which would be alarming if backward did not build a
fresh gradient from scratch every time it runs. Everything you learned about learning
rates in step zero carries over here untouched.

</Beat>

<Beat id="mlp-stack">

An MLP is a vector of layers, and once a layer is right this file is short.
`layer_sizes` gives the widths, and it has one more entry than there are layers,
because $n$ layers need $n+1$ shapes to sit between.

</Beat>

<Beat id="mlp-forward">

Forward is a loop. Take the input, hand it to the first layer, take what comes out,
hand that to the next. The one thing worth noticing is that it passes references
rather than copies. Each layer already owns the buffer holding its own output, so
there is nothing to copy, and at MNIST batch sizes that saves a lot of pointless
memory traffic.

</Beat>

<Beat id="mlp-backward">

Backward is the same loop running the other way, and `rbegin` is what makes it the
other way. Start at the last layer with the gradient of the loss, and each layer
hands the one behind it exactly what that layer needs. This is backpropagation, and
thats genuinely all it is: forward through the stack, then the chain rule back
through it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="mlp-linear">

There is one rule the constructor enforces, and it looks arbitrary until you connect
it to something. The last layer's activation has to be `LINEAR`. Remember that the
gradient we computed, $p - y$, already had the softmax folded into it. If the output
layer applied a softmax of its own as well, it would be applied twice, once going
forward and once inside the gradient. The last layer does nothing on purpose, because
the loss function is already doing it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="mlp-gradcheck">

One more file ships alongside this one, `grad_check.hpp`, and its the thing that
tells you whether any of the above is right. Nudge a single weight by a tiny amount,
measure how much the loss actually moves, and compare that against the gradient the
backward pass claims. If they agree to several decimal places your calculus is right.
If they do not, there is a sign error somewhere, and this is how you find it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-defaults">

The driver runs two datasets, and nearly every default depends on which one you pick.
XOR gets eight hidden units, tanh, a learning rate of one half, and two thousand
epochs, which sounds enormous until you remember the whole training set is four
examples. MNIST gets thirty two hidden units, ReLU, a tenth, and fifteen. These are
the numbers you will see filled in on the training page.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-xor-works">

So, XOR, with a real hidden layer doing the moving. On the left the four points as
they arrive. On the right where they land, and the line that now separates them
cleanly. One neuron managed seventy five percent on this. Two layers get all of it.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-mnist">

MNIST is the reason anyone builds one of these. Twenty eight by twenty eight
greyscale digits, and the network is seven hundred and eighty four inputs, thirty two
hidden units, ten outputs. The picture becomes one row of seven hundred and eighty
four doubles, read left to right and top to bottom. The network is never told that
neighbouring pixels are related. It gets a flat list of numbers, and it still works.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-batches">

Training does not run the whole set at once. It shuffles the row order, walks through
in batches of sixty four, and each batch gets one forward pass, one backward pass and
one update. Notice the shuffle sits inside the epoch loop, so the batches come out
different every time round. Same data, different groupings, and that stops the model
learning anything about the order.

</Beat>

<Beat id="main-loop">

And here it is, all of it, in one function. Shuffle. Take a batch. Forward through
the stack. Turn the logits into a gradient with that one line carrying the folded-in
softmax. Backward through the stack. Update every layer. Thats the entire training
loop, and every piece of it is something we built.

</Beat>
